PART 1

Where the money comes from

India has Members of Parliament. People elect them. There are around 788 of them across both houses.

Every MP gets ₹5 crore every year to spend on building useful things in the area that elected them. Roads. School buildings. Water pipes. Health centres. This scheme is called MPLADS.

Here is the important part. The MP never touches the money. The MP only suggests what should be built. Government officials in the district then do the actual work: they approve it, hire a contractor, and pay the bills.

Think of it like this

Imagine every class monitor in a school gets ₹5,000 a year to improve their own classroom.

The monitor does not get cash in hand. They write down what they want, like "we need three new fans." The school office checks it, finds a shop, and pays the shop directly.

So the monitor decides what. The office handles the money.

Now scale that up. Around 788 MPs, ₹5 crore each, every year, for decades. That is a very large amount of public money moving through thousands of small building projects all over the country.

Who is involved

WhoWhat they do
MPSuggests what should be built. Never handles money.
District AuthorityGovernment officials in the district. They approve the work, release money, and are supposed to go check that it was actually built.
Implementing agencyThe government body that carries out the work, like the roads department.
Contractor / vendorThe private company that actually builds it and gets paid.
MoSPIThe ministry in Delhi that runs the whole scheme nationally. This is who our software is for.

MoSPI stands for Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. You only need to remember that it is the ministry in charge, and that our user is a senior officer who works there.

PART 2

The rule nobody can follow properly

The scheme has its own rulebook. One rule in it matters more than anything else in this entire project.

Officials must physically go and inspect at least 10 out of every 100 ongoing works, every year.

Physically means actually going there. Standing at the site. Looking at whether the road exists and whether it looks like it cost what the paperwork says.

This is not our idea. It is already the law. It has been written down for years.

Here is the problem. The rule does not say which 10. There is no method, no list, no priority order. So officials pick more or less at random. And that means about 90 out of every 100 works are never visited by anybody, ever.

Think of it like this

A teacher has 100 homework notebooks and no time to check all of them. So the school rule says: check at least 10.

But the rule does not say which 10. So she picks whichever ones are on top of the pile.

Every student figures this out fast. Most of them will not get checked. So a few start doing sloppy work, or copying, because the odds are in their favour.

The teacher is following the rule perfectly. She checks 10. She is just checking the wrong 10.

This is the whole project

The problem is not that nobody is checking. Checking is already required by law. The problem is that the checking is aimed randomly. We are not adding a rule. We are aiming an existing one.

One more detail that helps your case. The rulebook already treats some works as higher risk: projects run through societies and trusts get inspected in full, not on the 10 percent sample. So the idea of "inspect riskier things more" is already accepted. We are just doing it with evidence instead of with categories.

PART 3

What we actually built

NidhiNetra looks at every ongoing work in the country and puts them in an order. Most worth inspecting at the top. Least worth inspecting at the bottom.

Then it draws a line at the 10 percent mark, because that is the legal minimum. Everything above the line is what the officer should go and see this year.

And next to every single work, it writes one plain sentence saying why it is on the list.

The name means "eye on the fund." Nidhi means fund. Netra means eye.

Back to the teacher

Instead of grabbing whichever notebooks are on top, the teacher now gets a sorted list.

"Check these 10 first. This one was submitted in two minutes when everyone else took an hour. This one has answers identical to the student sitting behind."

She still checks 10. Same effort, same rule. She is just checking the ones most worth checking, and she knows why each one is on the list.

Say this exactly, every time

It does not catch corruption. It says: "these are worth looking at first, and here is why." A human being makes every decision. We never accuse anyone of anything.

That sentence is not modesty and it is not a legal disclaimer. It is the most important design decision in the project, and the next part explains why.

PART 4

Why we say "odd" and never "wrong"

To teach a computer to spot cheating, you normally show it thousands of examples that a human has already marked. "This one was cheating. This one was not. This one was cheating." After enough examples, it learns the difference.

That is called supervised learning. Supervised, because a human supervised it by providing the answers.

We do not have that in the clean form a model would need. Nobody has built a spreadsheet where every MPLADS work has a row and a column saying "corrupt" or "fine." That does not exist.

What does exist: CAG audit reports and RTI-obtained case files, real government records that already name specific, confirmed instances of misuse in centrally-sponsored schemes -- inflated billing, works marked complete that were not, funds sitting idle for years, on-ground evidence that does not match the claim. Those are genuine, documented patterns of what misuse actually looks like, and they are not guesswork. They are confirmed cases, on the record, ahead of any live dataset -- which is exactly the feasibility argument: we are not inventing what to look for, we are building against patterns that already happened and were already confirmed.

We used those patterns to decide what to build detectors for: a work costing far more than similar works, a work receiving money but showing no progress for a year, one agency appearing across an unusual number of districts. Each check traces back to a real, confirmed way MPLADS-type money has actually been misused before.

Think of it like this

You are handed 100 notebooks and told to find the copied ones. Nobody hands you a marked answer key for these exact 100. But you have read old case files describing exactly how students cheated in years past: near-identical answers, finishing in two minutes flat, matching a neighbour's mistakes word for word. Those old cases tell you what to look for, even though none of them are one of these 100 notebooks.

So you check for the patterns those old cases taught you. If 99 students took an hour and one took two minutes, that matches a pattern you already know is worth checking. You still do not know, for certain, that this student copied. But you are not guessing blind either.

That is closer to unsupervised learning informed by known patterns than to fully supervised learning. The computer flags what does not fit the pattern of everything else, and the patterns it watches for were chosen because real, confirmed cases showed they matter.

This kind of method can tell you "this is unusual, and this is the kind of unusual that has mattered before." It genuinely cannot tell you "this specific work is wrong." Those are different statements, and the gap between them is why a human inspector still makes the decision.

The question that can sink you

If a judge asks "what is your model's accuracy? Precision, recall?", there is still no number, and you must not invent one. The CAG and RTI cases behind our detector design are real, but they are not joined, work by work, to today's live MPLADS records at a scale anything could be scored against. That is a different, more precise reason than "no data exists at all" -- know the difference, and give the precise one.

Here is the answer that works, and it is genuinely a strong one:

Say this

"There is no accuracy number today, because the confirmed CAG and RTI cases that shaped what we check for are not yet joined to live MPLADS records at a scale we could score against. What we can measure is whether inspectors, when they visit the works we ranked highest, find more problems than in a random sample. Every inspection our list triggers creates one of the labels we can build on at scale. The system builds its own answer key by being used, starting from patterns that were already confirmed real."

Read that last part again. A validated dataset at scale looks like something we are missing. Under our framing it turns into a plan we are already partway through: real, confirmed patterns first, then scale, through use. That reversal is one of the best things about this project, so be ready to say it.

PART 5

Comparing fairly

Say a road cost ₹40 lakh. Is that too much?

You cannot possibly know without asking: compared to what?

A road through mountains genuinely costs far more than the same length of road on flat ground. Rock has to be cut. Machinery has to be hauled up. Labour is harder to get. A mountain road at three times the price might be completely honest.

So we never compare a work against all works. We compare it only against works like it: same type, same state, same year. That set is called its peer group.

Think of it like this

You would never say a Class 2 student's handwriting is bad because it is worse than a Class 10 student's.

You compare Class 2 to Class 2. Then "this is unusual" actually means something.

Two rules protect this, and both matter in a Q&A.

One: we show the peer group on screen. Not hidden in the code. Printed right there, with its size: "Compared against 1,72,961 road works in Bihar, 2023-24." The officer can see exactly what the comparison was and can disagree with it.

Two: if the group is too small, we stay silent. Fewer than 30 comparable works and we do not flag it at all. Comparing something against four other things tells you nothing, and a confident sentence built on four examples is worse than saying nothing.

And we never quietly widen the group to reach 30. Widening "road works in Bihar in 2023-24" to "all works in India ever" would technically give us enough numbers, and would destroy the entire point.

This answers the hardest fairness question

When someone says "what about a hill road that genuinely costs three times more?", the answer is: we never compared it to a plains road. We compared it to other road works in its own state and year, and we printed that comparison on the screen so the officer can judge for themselves.

PART 6

Three ways to spot something odd

We use three methods together. Each catches a different kind of strange. You do not need the maths, only the idea behind each one.

Method 1 · The z-score

This one measures how far something sits from normal, counted in normal-sized steps.

Think of it like this

In a class, most students score between 60 and 70. That range is "normal," and a normal-sized step is about 5 marks.

Someone scores 95. That is roughly five steps above the middle. Very unusual.

It does not mean they cheated. Maybe they studied hard. But out of 100 papers, that is one of the ones you would look at.

We do exactly this with money. If road works in that state and year usually cost around ₹12 lakh, and one cost ₹40 lakh, that is far outside normal. Worth a look.

This method does most of the work, because it is the easiest to explain to a government officer. "It cost 3.2 times the typical road in your state this year" is a sentence anyone can argue with, which is exactly what makes it trustworthy.

Method 2 · Isolation Forest

Strange name, simple idea. It measures how easily something can be separated from the crowd.

Think of it like this

A hundred people stand in a field. You draw random straight lines across it, over and over, splitting people into smaller and smaller groups.

Someone standing in the middle of the crowd takes many lines before they end up alone. They are surrounded.

Someone standing off by themselves in a corner gets cut off by the very first line.

So: the fewer lines it takes to isolate you, the more unusual you are.

The computer does this thousands of times with the data and scores every work by how quickly it gets separated. It is good at catching works that are strange across several things at once, in a way a single measurement would miss.

Method 3 · Local Outlier Factor

This one catches things that look normal overall but are odd compared to their immediate neighbours.

Think of it like this

In a crowded market, everyone stands close together. If one person has a three-metre empty circle around them while everyone else is shoulder to shoulder, that stands out, even though they are still inside the market.

Looking at the whole market from above, nothing seems wrong. Looking at their little patch, something is.

Using all three together means a work has to look ordinary from three different angles to avoid being flagged.

What the submitted proposal also named

The idea-submission deck also named DBSCAN (a method for finding dense clusters, useful for spotting a tight ring of works or agencies) and NLP/computer-vision cross-checks: matching near-duplicate work descriptions, and comparing submitted photos against a work's claimed location. Both are real, useful techniques for this problem. Neither is running in this prototype -- they need a live, work-level dataset at real scale to be worth building, which is exactly the piece still being connected (see part 8). This prototype proves the core mechanism end to end first, with the three methods above, so the next methods have a working system to plug into rather than a slide.

What actually gets flagged

FlagIn plain words

Expenditure mismatchMoney released and money actually spent do not add up.

PART 7

The money network

Some patterns are invisible when you look at works one at a time. You only see them when you look at how everything connects.

So we draw a picture. Every MP is a dot. Every agency is a dot. Every contractor is a dot. When money flows between two of them, we draw a line.

In computing, a picture of dots joined by lines is called a graph or a network. It has nothing to do with bar charts.

Think of it like this

Every classroom in a school buys its own supplies. Individually, each purchase looks perfectly ordinary.

Then you draw lines from every classroom to every shop they bought from.

One shop has lines coming from forty different classrooms across every floor of the building. No single purchase looked odd. The pattern does.

That is what we look for: one contractor or agency showing up across far more MPs and districts than is normal. It might be a genuinely large, capable firm. Or it might be worth a look. Either way, no one can see it by reading a table.

Be honest about this limitation

This only means something at national scale. A single district has too few agencies and contractors for concentration to be meaningful. That is exactly why our user is the ministry in Delhi and not a district officer, even though the district officer has the more immediate problem.

PART 8

Where the data comes from

The government runs a public website showing MPLADS information. A website is meant for human eyes: you open a page and read it.

Our software needs thousands of records, so reading pages is useless. Instead we use an API, which is a way for one program to ask another program directly for data and get back a clean list.

Think of it like this

A website is a noticeboard. You walk up and read it with your eyes.

An API is a counter with a clerk behind it. You say "give me all road works in Bihar" and they hand you a neat list.

Same information. One is for people, one is for programs.

We checked this working on 1 September 2026. The government's own system returned the real national numbers you will see in part 10. Those figures are genuinely live, not invented.

Fresh data, but a safety net

We fetch new data regularly, and there is a button to fetch it right now. But government websites can be slow, and hackathon venue wifi is famously unreliable.

So we always keep the last successful copy. That saved copy is called a snapshot or a cache. If a fetch fails, the screen keeps showing the last good data and says how old it is, right there on screen. Never a blank page. Never an error message.

Think of it like this

You photograph the noticeboard every morning.

One day the board is missing. You still have yesterday's photo, so you are not stuck. But you must say out loud "this is from yesterday," or you will mislead someone.

One more careful bit. When new data arrives, we do not delete the old copy first. We prepare the new one completely, check it is not damaged, and only then swap it in, in one motion. If the new one turns out broken, the old one is untouched.

You will not be asked about that. But if a technical judge asks what happens when a download fails halfway, that is the answer, and it is the kind of detail that signals the system was built by someone careful.

PART 9

What is on the screen

One main screen. A list of works, most worth inspecting at the top.

On screenWhy it is there
Four summary figuresHow many works are ongoing, how many are flagged, and how much money is involved. The situation in one glance.
FiltersNarrow to a state, a year, a type of work.
The 10 percent lineA bar showing exactly where the legal minimum falls. "168 works here. Your 10 percent duty ends at number 17."
The ranked listEvery work in priority order, with a plain sentence underneath saying why.
The detail panelClick any work to see the full breakdown and its peer group.

The 10 percent line is the single best thing on the screen. It takes an abstract legal duty and turns it into a visible mark. Point at it during your demo.

Why nothing is ever green

In almost every dashboard ever made, green means good and safe.

We use no green anywhere. Not one pixel.

Because green would mean "this work has been checked and is fine," and we never checked anything. We looked at numbers on a screen. A work we did not flag is not clean. It is simply not yet looked at, which is a completely different thing.

Worth saying out loud in the demo

"There is no green in this interface, deliberately. Green would claim something we cannot prove. Nothing here is verified clean. It is either flagged, or not yet examined."

If a judge notices that on their own, it is a very good moment for you. It shows the honesty runs all the way down into the colour choices, not just the slides.

PART 11

If someone asks you

Question

How is this different from a normal government audit?

Audits look backwards. They examine a sample of what already happened, often years later, and tell you what went wrong. We do not audit anything. We decide which works get the physical visit that is already required this year. Different time, different purpose. And they work together: audit findings can later show whether our ranking was pointing at the right things.

Question

Why would any government department actually use this?

Because it asks nobody to do anything new. Officials already have to inspect 10 percent of works every year, and they already do it. They just choose which ones with no method at all. We sort a queue they are already legally required to work through. No new rule, no new process, no extra workload. That is about as easy as adoption gets.

Question

What if you flag an honest officer and ruin their reputation?

We never flag people. We flag works. No person is named as a suspect anywhere in the software. And think about what a mistake actually costs here: one site visit that was going to happen to some work anyway. We are changing which 10 percent gets visited. We are not adding inspections or investigations.

Question

Isn't this just another dashboard?

The existing government dashboard shows totals: money allocated, money spent. It answers "how are we doing overall." Ours answers a specific working question: out of 44,480 ongoing works, which 4,448 should someone physically go and see this year, in what order, and why each one. The screen is just how we deliver it. The ranking and the reason are the actual product.

Question

Could someone game it once they know how it works?

Partly, and we should admit that. If you know costs are compared within a group, you could keep every project just below the line. But two things limit it. We use several independent checks, so dodging the cost check can push you into the stalled-work or concentration check instead. And staying near the genuine average limits how much can be taken in the first place. Forcing dishonesty to stay small is a partial win.

Question

Where does your data come from? Is it real?

The government's own public MPLADS system. We tested it live and the national figures in part 10 came straight back from it. The detailed per-work information is the part we are still connecting. Say that plainly if asked. Overstating it is the fastest way to lose a judge who decides to check.

Question

What is genuinely new here?

Two things. Most teams build something that tries to catch fraud, which governments cannot actually deploy, because an accusation you cannot prove is a liability. We built something that aims an obligation that already exists, so it can be used on day one. And every single flag shows its comparison group. Most systems give you a score, and a score cannot be argued with, which means it cannot really be trusted. Ours gives a reason a human can push back on.

PART 12

Words to use and words to avoid

When people get nervous they reach for dramatic words. One wrong word turns a careful, defensible tool into an accusation you cannot back up.

Never say

  • Detected fraud
  • Corruption
  • Guilty, culprit, suspect
  • Caught, exposed
  • Proves, confirms
  • Verified clean, safe
  • Our accuracy is X percent

Say instead

  • Flagged for inspection
  • Statistically unusual
  • Worth looking at first
  • For human review
  • Suggests, indicates
  • Not yet examined
  • Built from confirmed CAG/RTI patterns, not yet scored at scale

And if you genuinely do not know something, the best available answer is: "I do not have that number in front of me and I would rather not guess."

In a project whose entire argument is about refusing to overclaim, refusing to overclaim on stage is not weakness. It is the most consistent thing you could possibly do.
